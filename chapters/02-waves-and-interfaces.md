## 2.1 Introduction and Maxwell's fundamental equations

In Chapter 1, we saw how the subwavelength structures of metasurfaces challenged rules that had seemed obvious in optics and electromagnetics for years. But if we want to design a metasurface ourselves, intuitive descriptions are no longer enough. We need the universe's shared language for electromagnetics: Maxwell's equations.

Don't worry! We will not simply line up a collection of dry mathematical formulas. We want to regard these equations as the “rules of the game”—rules that metasurfaces aim to manipulate to our advantage.

Before the main equations, let's review three key relations describing how a material behaves in electromagnetic fields. These are the material's **constitutive relations**:

$$
\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E,\qquad
\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H,\qquad
\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E.
$$

Here, $\varepsilon$ is electric permittivity, $\mu$ magnetic permeability, and $\sigma$ conductivity: intrinsic properties of the medium. In metasurfaces, our art is to design unit cells that set artificial values of permittivity and permeability to suit our needs—even negative values!

To simplify engineering analyses, we usually assume that fields vary harmonically in time: they oscillate at a specified frequency. With the **harmonic convention $e^{j\omega t}$**, we can replace the time derivative, $\partial/\partial t$, with multiplication by $j\omega$ and move from troublesome time-domain differential equations into the more comfortable **phasor or frequency domain**.

Now let's meet the four pillars of electromagnetics. Below, Maxwell's equations appear in differential time-domain, phasor frequency-domain, and integral forms:

### 1. Faraday–Lenz law: electric fields generated by changing magnetic fields

Differential form:

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}.
$$

Phasor form:

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-j\omega\mathbf B.
$$

Integral form:

$$
\oint_C\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\iint_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf s.
$$

### 2. Ampère–Maxwell law: magnetic fields generated by electric current and changing electric fields

Differential form:

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}.
$$

Phasor form:

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J+j\omega\mathbf D.
$$

Integral form:

$$
\oint_C\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=\iint_S\left(\mathbf J+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}\right)\cdot d\mathbf s.
$$

### 3. Electric Gauss law: electric fields originate from electric charges

Differential form:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho.
$$

Integral form:

$$
\oiint_S\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf s=\iiint_V\rho\,dV.
$$

### 4. Magnetic Gauss law: there are no magnetic monopoles

Differential form:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0.
$$

Integral form:

$$
\oiint_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf s=0.
$$

### 2.1.1 Maxwell's displacement current and its role in metasurface resonances

Perhaps the most fascinating part of the equations above is the little term James Clerk Maxwell added to Ampère's law: **displacement current**.

Maxwell noticed a major contradiction in classical physics. Basic vector calculus tells us that the divergence of a curl is always zero: $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf H)=0$. If we take the divergence of the old, classical Ampère law, $\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J$, we would conclude that $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$.

But wait! Charge continuity tells us that $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=-\partial\rho/\partial t$. The classical Ampère law therefore works only for steady currents, where charge does not change with time, and reaches a dead end in alternating-current circuits.

Maxwell's insight was that a new term had to be added to Ampère's law to resolve the contradiction. He defined the displacement-current density, $\mathbf J_d$, as follows:

$$
\mathbf J_d=\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}=j\omega\varepsilon\mathbf E.
$$

Ampère's law then took its complete form, $\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J+\partial\mathbf D/\partial t$.

**Why does this matter so much when we design metasurfaces?**

When designing metasurface unit cells, such as the split-ring resonators or SRRs from Chapter 1, we often encounter **capacitive gaps**. There is no conduction current, $\mathbf J$, across an ideal gap: it is not metal, and electrons cannot pass through the air or dielectric. Yet the strong high-frequency electric-field oscillation, $j\omega\mathbf E$, produces a strong displacement current.

That displacement current completes the circuit and allows inductive–capacitive, $LC$, resonances to appear at nanometer or micrometer scales. The artificial polarization, abrupt phase changes, and unusual resonant responses we see in metasurfaces owe their existence to this invisible but essential Maxwell current!

:::{note} How broad are these constitutive laws?
The scalar relations above assume an appropriate linear, local, isotropic description. Linear does not imply nondispersive, homogeneous, or time invariant. A patterned sheet also need not admit a meaningful bulk refractive index. Electric field lines that begin and end on charges describe the electrostatic picture; time-varying electric fields can have circulation. Zero magnetic divergence excludes magnetic monopoles, but field lines may also extend to infinity rather than forming finite closed loops.
:::

## 2.2 Sources and field lines in unit-cell design

To understand metasurfaces physically and see how they manipulate waves, we need a clear picture of sources and the behavior of electric and magnetic field lines.

According to **electric Gauss law**, the divergence of electric flux density is directly related to electric charge density:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho.
$$

Or in integral form:

$$
\oiint_S\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf s=\iiint_V\rho\,dV.
$$

The net flux through any closed surface equals the electric charge enclosed in its volume. Therefore:

- Positive charges act as **sources of electric field**.
- Negative charges act as **sinks of electric field**.
- In simple terms, electric field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges.

By contrast, **magnetic Gauss law** states:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0.
$$

Or in integral form:

$$
\oiint_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf s=0.
$$

This relation tells us that the net magnetic flux through any closed surface is always zero. Consequently:

- Unlike electric field lines, magnetic field lines have no starting or ending point and form closed loops.
- This expresses the absence of **magnetic monopoles** in classical physics: there is no isolated magnetic pole acting alone as a source or sink of magnetic field.

### Sources and dipoles in metasurface unit-cell engineering

This fundamental difference between electric and magnetic fields is a cornerstone of metasurface analysis and design:

**1. Electric response: an effective electric dipole.** When an incident electromagnetic wave reaches a unit cell such as a metal patch or narrow strip, the metal's free charges move, forming oppositely signed surface-charge distributions at the element's ends. This separation acts like an effective electric dipole, whose moment is defined by:

$$
\mathbf p=\iiint_V\mathbf r\,\rho(\mathbf r)\,dV.
$$

For a thin planar structure, the distribution is described by a surface charge, $\rho_s$, on the metal or material interface.

**2. Magnetic response: an effective magnetic dipole.** Time-varying induced currents flowing along curved or loop-shaped metal paths, such as an SRR, produce current loops that behave like effective magnetic dipoles. The magnetic dipole moment of the current distribution is calculated as:

$$
\mathbf m=\frac12\iiint_V\mathbf r\times\mathbf J(\mathbf r)\,dV.
$$

For a simple current loop with current $I$ and vector area $\mathbf S$, this reduces to:

$$
\mathbf m=I\mathbf S.
$$

The area vector's direction follows the right-hand rule.

### The unit cell's physical operation, gathered together

In metasurfaces, we view each cell as an element combining induced electric and magnetic dipoles:

- **Charge distributions** determine electric response and polarization.
- **Circulating and loop currents** produce artificial magnetic response.

By changing geometry—patch dimensions, gap widths, dielectric-substrate thickness, element rotation and period—we can adjust these dipoles' amplitudes and phases. This precise local control lets us redesign a wavefront, control reflection and transmission, change polarization, or steer and focus a beam at a desired angle.

## 2.3 Properties and types of electromagnetic media

In electromagnetic analysis, field behavior depends strongly on the medium in which the fields propagate. Imagine running: an asphalt road, a sandy beach, and water certainly do not feel the same! Electromagnetic waves face the same sort of distinction. A material's intrinsic permittivity, $\varepsilon$, magnetic permeability, $\mu$, and conductivity, $\sigma$, determine how the wave behaves there.

We can therefore classify electromagnetic media from several perspectives:

### 2.3.1 Linear and nonlinear media

- **Linear medium:** the simplest and best-behaved case. Its material parameters, $\varepsilon$, $\mu$, and $\sigma$, are fixed and do not depend on electric- or magnetic-field strength. In simple terms, doubling the field doubles the response. The field-to-flux relationships are straightforward and linear:

$$
D=\varepsilon E,\qquad B=\mu H,\qquad J=\sigma E.
$$

- **Nonlinear medium:** what if increasing field strength changes the medium's behavior? Here material parameters depend directly on the applied field's amplitude and intensity. The relationships no longer form straight lines, and exciting effects such as **harmonic generation** can occur, with many applications in nonlinear optics and advanced metasurfaces.

::::{admonition} Textbook example: a linear material response
:class: book-exercise

A linear medium has permittivity $\varepsilon=4\varepsilon_0$. If its electric field increases to $E=3E_0$, find the new electric flux density in terms of $\varepsilon_0$ and $E_0$.

:::{dropdown} Show / hide answer
For a linear medium:

$$
D=\varepsilon E.
$$

Therefore:

$$
D=(4\varepsilon_0)(3E_0).
$$

Consequently:

$$
\boxed{D=12\varepsilon_0E_0}.
$$

Tripling the field also triples the electric flux density.
:::
::::

### 2.3.2 Homogeneous and inhomogeneous media

- **Homogeneous medium:** its fundamental parameters are identical everywhere and do not depend on position, $(x,y,z)$.
- **Inhomogeneous medium:** its physical properties change from one point to another. In mathematical terms, the parameters are functions of position rather than fixed numbers:

$$
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(x,y,z),\qquad
\mu=\mu(x,y,z),\qquad
\sigma=\sigma(x,y,z).
$$

:::{tip} Metasurface design connection
Creating this “artificial inhomogeneity” is one of the key techniques in metasurface design. By intelligently placing different cells beside one another, we create an inhomogeneous arrangement that bends or reshapes the wavefront as desired!
:::

::::{admonition} Textbook example: is the medium homogeneous?
:class: book-exercise

A medium has $\varepsilon(x,y,z)=\varepsilon_0(2+x)$. Is it homogeneous or inhomogeneous?

:::{dropdown} Show / hide answer
It is inhomogeneous because its permittivity depends on $x$. Even if $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are fixed, spatial dependence of one material parameter is enough to make the medium inhomogeneous.
:::
::::

### 2.3.3 Isotropic and anisotropic media

- **Isotropic medium:** no direction is preferred. Its properties are the same in every direction, so $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ can be expressed as simple scalars.
- **Anisotropic medium:** its response depends on the direction of the applied field. Walking through it is like walking through a forest whose clear paths run only north to south! Permittivity and permeability must now be described as tensors, or matrices:

$$
\mathbf D=\overline{\overline\varepsilon}\mathbf E,
\qquad
\begin{bmatrix}D_x\\D_y\\D_z\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}
\varepsilon_{xx}&\varepsilon_{xy}&\varepsilon_{xz}\\
\varepsilon_{yx}&\varepsilon_{yy}&\varepsilon_{yz}\\
\varepsilon_{zx}&\varepsilon_{zy}&\varepsilon_{zz}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}E_x\\E_y\\E_z\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This tensor behavior gives engineers remarkable freedom to control waves in complicated media.

::::{admonition} Textbook example: an anisotropic response
:class: book-exercise

The medium obeys:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}D_x\\D_y\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}2\varepsilon_0&0\\0&5\varepsilon_0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}E_x\\E_y\end{bmatrix}.
$$

If the field points only along $x$, find $D_x$ and $D_y$.

:::{dropdown} Show / hide answer
Matrix multiplication gives:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}D_x\\D_y\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}2\varepsilon_0&0\\0&5\varepsilon_0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}E_0\\0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Thus:

$$
D_x=2\varepsilon_0E_0,\qquad D_y=0.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{D_x=2\varepsilon_0E_0,\qquad D_y=0}.
$$

The response coefficients along $x$ and $y$ differ, so the medium is anisotropic.
:::
::::

### 2.3.4 Lossless and lossy media

- **Lossless medium:** a communication engineer's paradise! This ideal medium dissipates no electromagnetic energy as heat. Its conductivity is zero, $\sigma=0$, and its permittivity and permeability have no imaginary parts.
- **Lossy medium:** the case we usually encounter in practice. It has electrical conductivity, $\sigma>0$, or dielectric/magnetic losses represented by imaginary material parameters. The wave gradually loses energy and becomes weaker as it propagates.

::::{admonition} Textbook example: which medium is lossless?
:class: book-exercise

Which of these media is lossless?

A) $\sigma=0$, $\varepsilon=\varepsilon'$ and $\mu=\mu'$.

B) $\sigma=0.01\,\mathrm{S/m}$.

C) $\sigma=0$, $\varepsilon=\varepsilon'-j\varepsilon''$, with nonzero $\varepsilon''$.

:::{dropdown} Show / hide answer
Option A is lossless: conductivity is zero and the material parameters have no imaginary parts. B and C are lossy:

- In B, conductivity is positive.
- In C, permittivity has a nonzero imaginary part.
:::
::::

### 2.3.5 Time-invariant and time-varying media

- **Time-invariant medium:** its material properties do not depend on time. Perform an experiment today and repeat it tomorrow, and the medium behaves in the same way.
- **Time-varying medium:** its parameters change with time, $t$. Imagine a metasurface whose properties change almost instantaneously, in a fraction of a second, perhaps under an applied voltage: this is the frontier of programmable, time-varying metasurfaces!

## 2.4 Boundary conditions between two media

The differential form of Maxwell's equations describes fields at a continuous point. To find a unique solution to an electromagnetic problem, we must also specify its initial and boundary conditions. Without them, a problem may have infinitely many solutions. In microwave and metasurface engineering, boundary conditions therefore matter as much as the governing equations: the boundaries determine the final field response and how the wave behaves.

Boundary conditions relate fields on either side of an interface. It can be a real interface between two materials, such as air and a dielectric, or an ideal artificial boundary used in a simulation. We usually decompose each field at the interface into two components, as in Figure 1:

- **Normal component, $F_n$:** perpendicular to the surface; its relations follow from the divergence equations, or Gauss laws.
- **Tangential component, $F_t$:** in the interface plane; its relations follow from the curl equations, Faraday and Ampère laws.

```{figure} ../assets/chapter-02/textbook-normal-tangential.svg
:alt: Redrawing of textbook page 22: the field at a two-medium boundary is resolved into normal and tangential components.

Textbook Figure 1: field decomposition at a real or artificial boundary. The vertical arrow is the normal component, $F_n$, the arrow along the interface is $F_t$, and the diagonal arrow is the total field, $\mathbf F$. The picture applies to $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf H$, $\mathbf D$, and $\mathbf B$. Original book redrawing based on source page 22.
```

### 2.4.1 Deriving boundary relations for normal components

To find the normal electric-flux-density relation, we apply the integral form of Gauss law, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho$, to a tiny cylindrical volume—a Gaussian pillbox—straddling the interface, as in Figure 2:

$$
\oiint_S\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf s=Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\iint_{\mathrm{down}}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf s
+\iint_{\mathrm{up}}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf s=\rho_s\Delta S.
$$

```{figure} ../assets/chapter-02/textbook-gauss-pillbox.svg
:alt: A Gaussian pillbox straddles the boundary; outward area vectors point up at the upper cap and down at the lower cap.

Textbook Figure 2: Gaussian pillbox with one cap in each medium. The cap arrows indicate opposite outward area directions; normal field components and the interface normal are also shown. Original redrawing of source page 23.
```

To obtain a relation exactly at the interface, shrink the cylinder height to zero, $h\to0$. Its side area vanishes, so no finite side flux remains. Taking $\hat{\mathbf n}$ from medium 2 toward medium 1, with $d\mathbf s_1=\Delta S\hat{\mathbf n}$ and $d\mathbf s_2=-\Delta S\hat{\mathbf n}$, converts the cap integrals into products:

$$
-D_{n2}\Delta S+D_{n1}\Delta S=\rho_s\Delta S
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\mathbf D_1-\mathbf D_2)=D_{n1}-D_{n2}=\rho_s.
$$

The jump in normal electric flux density is exactly the free surface-charge density, $\rho_s$. Figure 3 shows how different charges affect the field lines: positive surface charge, $\rho_s>0$, makes the normal component stronger in medium 1 and directs field lines outward from the surface.

```{figure} ../assets/chapter-02/textbook-surface-charge.svg
:alt: Positive, zero and negative surface-charge cases; field-line density differs above and below the surface according to charge sign.

Textbook Figure 3: from left to right, $\rho_s>0$, $\rho_s=0$, and $\rho_s<0$. The normal-field jump is related to surface charge. Original three-panel redrawing of source page 23.
```

Similarly, because there are no magnetic monopoles, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$, there is no magnetic surface charge, and normal magnetic flux density is continuous:

$$
B_{n1}=B_{n2}.
$$

### 2.4.2 Deriving boundary relations for tangential components

To find the tangential electric-field condition, apply Faraday–Lenz induction to a closed rectangular contour straddling the interface, as in Figure 4:

$$
\oint_C\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\iint_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf s.
$$

```{figure} ../assets/chapter-02/textbook-electric-loop.svg
:alt: A rectangular loop straddles the boundary; opposite horizontal segments have opposite integration directions, with half-heights indicated on the vertical segments.

Textbook Figure 4: a thin Faraday-law loop with horizontal length $a$ and total height $h$. Contributions on opposite sides carry signs from the contour direction. Original redrawing of source page 24.
```

Integrating around a rectangle of width $a$ and height $h$, remembering that opposite segments have opposite directed lengths, gives:

$$
E_{x1}a+E_{y1}\frac h2+E_{y2}\frac h2
-E_{x2}a-E_{y1}\frac h2-E_{y2}\frac h2
=-\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}(ha).
$$

For a finite rate of magnetic-flux change, $\partial B/\partial t$, shrinking the loop height to zero makes the right-hand side vanish. Terms involving $h/2$ vanish as well. Consequently:

$$
E_{x1}=E_{x2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf E_1-\mathbf E_2)=0.
$$

The tangential electric field is therefore continuous across the interface. Figure 5 compares correct and incorrect representations of that continuity.

```{figure} ../assets/chapter-02/textbook-tangential-continuity.svg
:alt: Three panels compare tangential-field continuity: matching components in the first, mismatched components in the second and third.

Textbook Figure 5: the correct case is on the left; the middle and right cases are incorrect. The tangential components must agree, rather than the entire field vectors. Original redrawing of source page 24.
```

Now consider the tangential magnetic field, $\mathbf H$. Applying Ampère–Maxwell law to a similar contour, Figure 6, gives:

$$
\oint_C\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=\iint_S\left(\mathbf J+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}\right)\cdot d\mathbf s.
$$

```{figure} ../assets/chapter-02/textbook-magnetic-loop.svg
:alt: Rectangular magnetic-field integration loop across a boundary, with dots representing surface current normal to the drawing plane.

Textbook Figure 6: Ampère loop for the magnetic field and surface-current sheet. Dots indicate current directed out of the drawing plane. Original redrawing of source page 25.
```

Expanding the contour integral as before and taking $h\to0$ removes the displacement-current contribution, $\partial D/\partial t$. At an ordinary dielectric interface, the conduction-current density is finite, so its contribution also vanishes, giving $H_{x1}=H_{x2}$: continuity of the field.

If the lower medium is a conductor, current can instead concentrate into an infinitely thin layer as $h$ approaches zero. We then obtain a **surface-current density, $\mathbf J_s$**:

$$
\mathbf J_s=\lim_{h\to0}(\mathbf J h).
$$

The equation now becomes:

$$
H_{x1}-H_{x2}=J_s.
$$

Its general vector form, with $\hat{\mathbf n}$ pointing from medium 2 to medium 1, is:

$$
\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf H_1-\mathbf H_2)=\mathbf J_s.
$$

### 2.4.3 Ideal boundaries and their metasurface connection

If medium 2 is a perfect electric conductor, or PEC, time-varying electromagnetic fields do not penetrate it, and the fields inside are zero: $\mathbf H_2=0$ and $\mathbf E_2=0$. The neighboring surface-current relation then takes its simplest form:

$$
\mathbf J_s=\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf H_1.
$$

At a PEC boundary, such as a ground plane behind a metasurface, the induced surface-current density is determined directly by the tangential magnetic field outside the conductor.

:::{tip} Key connection
By patterning metasurface cells, we manipulate these surface currents, $\mathbf J_s$, and surface-charge distributions, $\rho_s$, to create the field discontinuities needed to control a wavefront.
:::

### Corrected summary table: boundary relations to keep beside us

Let's gather the derivation results in one table. The normal $\hat{\mathbf n}$ points from medium 2 to medium 1; $\rho_s$ and $\mathbf J_s$ are free surface charge and current. The media are linear and isotropic, and **there is no magnetic surface current in this table**. The PEC column concerns time-varying fields with zero interior field.

We also use tangential electric-field continuity only when magnetic surface current is absent; a magnetic equivalent sheet or GSTC model requires reconsidering that assumption. Across an artificial boundary within one unchanged material, all components are continuous when there are no surface sources, so that case needs no repeated column.

:::{dropdown} Show / hide answer
First resolve the known field, $\mathbf E_2$, into tangential components in the $xy$ plane and a normal component along $z$:

$$
\mathbf E_{2t}=(3,0,0),\qquad \mathbf E_{2n}=(0,0,-4).

\mathbf E_{1t}=\mathbf E_{2t}=(3,0,0).

Next consider the normal component. Since $\rho_s=0$, Gauss law requires normal electric flux density to be continuous:

$$
\mathbf D_{1n}=\mathbf D_{2n}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\varepsilon_1\mathbf E_{1n}=\varepsilon_2\mathbf E_{2n}.
$$

Substituting $\varepsilon=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$ gives:

$$
\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r1}\mathbf E_{1n}
=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{r2}\mathbf E_{2n}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 4\mathbf E_{1n}=2(0,0,-4).
$$

$$
\mathbf E_{1n}=\frac24(0,0,-4)=(0,0,-2)\,\mathrm{V/m}.
$$

Finally, combine the normal and tangential components to obtain the electric field in medium 1:

$$
\boxed{\mathbf E_1=\mathbf E_{1t}+\mathbf E_{1n}=(3,0,-2)\,\mathrm{V/m}}.
$$

As the physics requires, the tangential $x$ component passes through unchanged. The normal $z$ component changes because the permittivity changes, and its magnitude decreases.

### 2. Ideal boundaries: playing with PEC and PMC

When analyzing and simulating metasurface unit cells, we often use two hypothetical ideal boundaries:

**A) Perfect electric conductor, PEC:** a medium with infinite conductivity, $\sigma\to\infty$, in which the electric field approaches zero.

- **Boundary condition:** $\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf E=0$; tangential electric field is zero.
- **Reflection:** waves return with a $180^\circ$ phase change, $\Gamma=-1$. This is the ground plane we commonly place behind reflective metasurfaces.

**B) Perfect magnetic conductor, PMC:** this surface does not exist in nature, but it is the mathematical dual of a PEC and has infinite impedance.

- **Boundary condition:** $\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf H=0$; tangential magnetic field is zero.
- **Reflection:** waves return without a phase change, $\Gamma=+1$. Designing a high-impedance surface, or HIS, is an attempt to create an artificial PMC.

### 3. Real-world boundaries: the Leontovich condition, SIBC

Real metals, such as copper and gold, are not perfect conductors; their conductivity is finite, $\sigma<\infty$. The electromagnetic wave penetrates a short distance into the metal, called the skin depth and denoted by $\delta$.

The **Leontovich surface-impedance boundary condition** connects the tangential fields using the surface impedance, $Z_s$:

$$
\mathbf E_t=Z_s(\hat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf H_t).
$$

Surface impedance is complex:

$$
Z_s=R_s+jX_s=\sqrt{\frac{j\omega\mu}{\sigma}}.
$$

- **Surface resistance, $R_s$:** represents ohmic loss—the effect we seek in an absorbing metasurface.
- **Surface reactance, $X_s$:** represents near-field storage of electric, capacitive energy or magnetic, inductive energy.

### 4. A preview of metasurfaces: generalized sheet transition conditions

The cases above concerned classical homogeneous surfaces. Modern metasurfaces can be **inhomogeneous and graded**. To analyze them, we extend classical boundary relations into **generalized sheet transition conditions, GSTCs**. The field discontinuities, $\Delta\mathbf E$ and $\Delta\mathbf H$, connect directly to the unit cells' electric and magnetic dipole moments. This is precisely where metasurface engineering begins!

## 2.5 Plane waves

After Maxwell's equations and boundary conditions, the next step is one of their most important solutions: the **plane wave**. It is the simplest and most fundamental model for electromagnetic propagation in a homogeneous medium. In many metasurface problems, the initial illumination is modeled as a plane wave, and reflected, transmitted and scattered fields are analyzed using the same representation.

A plane wave has parallel planar equiphase surfaces. At a given instant, field phase is constant across a specified plane and changes only along the propagation direction. For propagation along $z$, the field depends only on $z$ and $t$. We can then express it as the sum of two traveling waves:

$$
\mathbf E(z,t)=\mathbf E^+(z,t)+\mathbf E^-(z,t).
$$

Here $\mathbf E^+$ propagates along $+z$, and $\mathbf E^-$ along $-z$.

This representation matters because illumination of a surface can often be decomposed into plane waves traveling in different directions: its plane-wave spectrum. Amplitude, phase, propagation vector and the electric-to-magnetic field relation therefore underpin reflection, transmission, absorption, scattering and polarization-conversion analysis.

### 2.5.1 The electromagnetic wave equation

In a linear, homogeneous, isotropic, time-invariant medium without free sources, Maxwell's equations are:

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\mu\frac{\partial\mathbf H}{\partial t},
\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf H=\sigma\mathbf E+\varepsilon\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.
$$

Here $\varepsilon$ is permittivity, $\mu$ permeability, and $\sigma$ conductivity. In a lossless medium, set $\sigma=0$, simplifying Ampère–Maxwell law to:

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf H=\varepsilon\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.
$$

To obtain the electric-field wave equation, take the curl of Faraday's law:

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)
=-\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\mathbf H).
$$

Substituting Ampère–Maxwell law gives:

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)
=-\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.
$$

The vector identity also tells us:

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)
=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E.
$$

Without free charge, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0$. The assumed homogeneous medium obeys $\mathbf D=\varepsilon\mathbf E$, giving $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0$. The identity therefore simplifies to:

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=-\nabla^2\mathbf E.
$$

Equating the two results yields the **electric-field wave equation**:

$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.
$$

Since the wave speed is $v=1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}$, we can write its standard form:

$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E=\frac1{v^2}\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.
$$

Exactly the same reasoning gives the magnetic-field equation:

$$
\nabla^2\mathbf H=\frac1{v^2}\frac{\partial^2\mathbf H}{\partial t^2}.
$$

These relations show that electric and magnetic fields propagate as waves in the absence of free sources.

In free space, propagation speed equals the speed of light:

$$
v=c=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}\approx3\times10^8\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$

In a material medium, the propagation speed is:

$$
v=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}}=\frac c{\sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}}.
$$

Most nonmagnetic dielectric design materials have $\mu_r\approx1$, so:

$$
v\approx\frac c{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}.
$$

:::{tip} Key design connection
Changing substrate permittivity changes the effective wavelength, accumulated phase and unit-cell resonance dimensions. This makes the relation especially important in metasurface design.
:::

### 2.5.2 A one-dimensional wave-equation solution

For simplicity, suppose the field varies spatially only along $z$. Each Cartesian component, $i=x,y,z$, then obeys:

$$
\frac{\partial^2 E_i(z,t)}{\partial z^2}
=\frac1{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 E_i(z,t)}{\partial t^2}.
$$

The general solution is the sum of two traveling waves:

$$
E_i(z,t)=f_i(z-vt)+g_i(z+vt).
$$

The function $f_i(z-vt)$ travels along $+z$, while $g_i(z+vt)$ travels along $-z$.

For a sinusoidal, harmonic wave, write:

$$
E_i(z,t)=A_i\sin\left[\omega\left(t-\frac zv\right)\right]
+B_i\sin\left[\omega\left(t+\frac zv\right)\right].
$$

Or in the more familiar form:

$$
E_i(z,t)=A_i\sin(\omega t-\beta z)+B_i\sin(\omega t+\beta z).
$$

Here:

$$
\beta=\frac\omega v.
$$

We call $\beta$ the **phase constant**, or wavenumber: it measures phase change per unit length. It is related to wavelength by:

$$
\beta=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\lambda=\frac{2\pi}{\beta}.
$$

The relation between phase speed, angular frequency and phase constant is:

$$
v=\frac\omega\beta.
$$

Using $\omega=2\pi f$ gives:

$$
v=f\lambda.
$$

These relations matter because the unit cell's **subwavelength condition** usually refers to the effective wavelength inside the medium, not just the free-space wavelength. On a high-permittivity substrate, the effective wavelength decreases, so the resonant dimensions must be chosen smaller as well.

### 2.5.3 The plane-wave phasor representation

Microwave frequency-domain analysis represents sinusoidal fields with phasors. The source book uses $e^{j\omega t}$. The real field corresponding to a vector phasor, $\mathbf E(z)$, is:

$$
\mathbf E(z,t)=\operatorname{Re}\{\mathbf E(z)e^{j\omega t}\}.
$$

The operator $\operatorname{Re}$ takes the real part. With this time convention, a plane wave traveling along $+z$ has phasor:

$$
\mathbf E(z)=\mathbf E_0e^{-j\beta z}.
$$

Its corresponding real field is:

$$
\mathbf E(z,t)=\operatorname{Re}\{\mathbf E_0e^{-j\beta z}e^{j\omega t}\}.
$$

By contrast, a wave traveling along $-z$ has phasor:

$$
\mathbf E(z)=\mathbf E_0e^{+j\beta z}.
$$

The spatial phase sign therefore determines propagation direction. With $e^{j\omega t}$, $e^{-j\beta z}$ travels along $+z$ and $e^{+j\beta z}$ travels along $-z$.

This convention is retained throughout the source book's subsequent wave-impedance, reflection/transmission, surface-impedance and GSTC relations.

### 2.5.4 Electric and magnetic fields in a plane wave

In a **uniform plane wave**, electric field, magnetic field and propagation direction are mutually perpendicular. For propagation along the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf k}$:

$$
\mathbf H=\frac1\eta\hat{\mathbf k}\times\mathbf E.
$$

Equivalently:

$$
\mathbf E=\eta\mathbf H\times\hat{\mathbf k}.
$$

Here $\eta$ is the medium's **intrinsic impedance**, found from:

$$
\eta=\sqrt{\frac\mu\varepsilon}.
$$

In free space:

$$
\eta_0=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}}\approx120\pi\,\Omega\approx377\,\Omega.
$$

For a nonmagnetic dielectric, $\mu_r\approx1$, intrinsic impedance is:

$$
\eta=\frac{\eta_0}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}.
$$

The ratio of electric-field amplitude to magnetic-field amplitude therefore depends only on the medium:

$$
\frac{|\mathbf E|}{|\mathbf H|}=\eta.
$$

As a wave enters a different medium, a change in intrinsic impedance can cause a mismatch and therefore reflection. This simple principle is the **physical foundation of impedance-matching metasurfaces, absorbers, and antireflection coatings**.

In a uniform plane wave, the perpendicularity relations are:

$$
\mathbf E\perp\mathbf H,\qquad
\mathbf E\perp\hat{\mathbf k},\qquad
\mathbf H\perp\hat{\mathbf k}.
$$

Thus $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf H$, and $\hat{\mathbf k}$ form a **right-handed system**, and both fields are **transverse** to propagation.

### 2.5.5 A plane wave in a lossy medium

In a lossless medium, the propagation constant contains only a phase term, $\gamma=j\beta$. In a lossy medium, amplitude decreases as well as phase changing. We define a complex propagation constant:

$$
\gamma=\alpha+j\beta.
$$

Here $\alpha$ is the **attenuation constant**, measured in nepers per meter, $\mathrm{Np/m}$, and $\beta$ is the phase constant, measured in $\mathrm{rad/m}$.

For propagation along $\hat{\mathbf k}$, the propagation factor becomes:

$$
e^{-\gamma\hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\mathbf r}
=e^{-\alpha\hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\mathbf r}
e^{-j\beta\hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\mathbf r}.
$$

The first, real exponential describes amplitude decay along propagation; the second, imaginary exponential describes phase change. A plane-wave field in a lossy medium can therefore be written:

$$
\mathbf F(\mathbf r)=\hat{\mathbf a}_F F_0e^{-\gamma\hat{\mathbf k}\cdot\mathbf r}.
$$

The field $\mathbf F$ can be electric or magnetic, and the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf a}_F$ specifies its polarization.

Loss, whether ohmic or dielectric, can be desirable in an absorber because it turns wave energy into heat and reduces reflection. In a highly efficient transmissive or reflective metasurface, however, loss is generally undesirable because it wastes power that could have entered the useful outgoing wave.

### 2.5.6 Connecting plane waves to metasurface design

Plane waves are the basic language of metasurface analysis. Many designs specify an incident plane wave with a given amplitude, phase, polarization and incidence angle:

$$
\mathbf E_i(\mathbf r)=\mathbf E_{0i}e^{-j\mathbf k_i\cdot\mathbf r}.
$$

Its interaction with the surface produces reflected and transmitted fields, which can also be represented using plane-wave spectra:

$$
\mathbf E_r(\mathbf r)=\mathbf E_{0r}e^{-j\mathbf k_r\cdot\mathbf r},
\qquad
\mathbf E_t(\mathbf r)=\mathbf E_{0t}e^{-j\mathbf k_t\cdot\mathbf r}.
$$

Uniform metasurfaces usually follow ordinary reflection and Snell refraction laws. In a phase-gradient surface, gradual structural variation can change the tangential wavevector, allowing the reflected or transmitted direction to be engineered through generalized Snell's law.

Each **unit cell** must give the incident plane wave a specified local response. That may mean changing phase, amplitude, absorption, polarization or propagation direction, or creating a desired near-field or far-field distribution.

We therefore needed a precise plane-wave framework before introducing reflection/transmission coefficients, surface impedance and GSTCs. A plane wave is the standard input to many metasurface problems; the surface's response is interpreted through its changes to that wave's amplitude, phase, polarization and direction.

## 2.6 Frequency-domain wave analysis

As we saw in the phasor discussion, many electromagnetic problems, especially metasurface designs, examine fields at one or several specified operating frequencies. Using $e^{j\omega t}$ turns time derivatives into multiplication by $j\omega$. This simple trick replaces complicated time-domain differential equations with much more familiar algebraic frequency-domain relations.

Under this assumption, the Maxwell curl equations in a linear, homogeneous, isotropic medium become:

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-j\omega\mu\mathbf H,
\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf H=(\sigma+j\omega\varepsilon)\mathbf E.
$$

Here $\sigma$ is conductivity, $\varepsilon$ permittivity, and $\mu$ permeability. The quantity $\sigma+j\omega\varepsilon$ can be viewed as an effective complex bulk admittivity: it combines conduction current, representing ohmic loss, and displacement current, representing dielectric response.

Frequency-domain analysis is the shared language of reference books and simulation packages such as CST and HFSS. It lets us include substrate and metal losses directly in wave-propagation parameters.

## 2.7 Key parameters in material and lossy media

In real media, such as FR4 or Rogers substrates and gold or copper metasurface layers, waves do more than change phase: they lose energy too. Section 2.5 described this with $\gamma=\alpha+j\beta$. To understand the physics of loss better, let's add several key parameters to our design toolbox.

### 2.7.1 Complex permittivity and loss tangent

In a real material, dielectric polarization responds with a small lag behind the electric field. This phase lag converts some wave energy into heat. We model it using complex permittivity:

$$
\varepsilon^*=\varepsilon'-j\varepsilon''.
$$

The real part, $\varepsilon'$, describes the material's ability to store electric energy; the imaginary loss parameter, $\varepsilon''$, describes dissipation associated with internal damping.

If conductivity is present as well as dielectric loss, we can gather both into an equivalent complex permittivity:

$$
\varepsilon^*=\varepsilon'-j\left(\varepsilon''+\frac\sigma\omega\right).
$$

For microwave engineers, the most tangible loss measure is the **loss tangent**: the ratio of total dissipative response to the real, energy-storage response:

$$
\tan\delta=\frac{\varepsilon''+\sigma/\omega}{\varepsilon'}.
$$

The smaller $\tan\delta$ is, with $\tan\delta\ll1$, the closer the material is to an ideal dielectric. A very low-loss-tangent substrate is one of the main ingredients in a highly efficient metasurface.

### 2.7.2 Complex intrinsic impedance

In a lossless medium, wave impedance is real. In a lossy medium it becomes complex:

$$
\eta=\sqrt{\frac{j\omega\mu}{\sigma+j\omega\varepsilon}}.
$$

A complex impedance means that electric and magnetic fields are no longer exactly in phase, reminding us that the medium absorbs wave energy. In metamaterial absorbers, carefully matching this complex response to free-space impedance is a key to **perfect absorption**.

### 2.7.3 Skin depth

A wave's amplitude decreases as it advances into a lossy material. The distance over which amplitude falls to $1/e$, about 37% of its original value, is its penetration or **skin depth, $\delta$**:

$$
\delta=\frac1\alpha.
$$

For a good conductor, such as copper or gold in the suitable regime, $\sigma\gg\omega\varepsilon$, and the familiar skin-depth approximation is:

$$
\delta\approx\sqrt{\frac2{\omega\mu\sigma}}.
$$

This has an important engineering message: at high frequencies such as microwave and terahertz frequencies, waves penetrate only a small distance into metal. Induced currents concentrate near the surface, in layers on micrometer or nanometer scales. Metasurface metal layers are therefore usually made somewhat thicker than several skin depths.

### 2.7.4 Complex refractive index in optics

If you are interested in optical metasurfaces, you probably know that optics and photonics often use refractive index rather than permittivity and permeability. In a lossy medium, index also becomes complex:

$$
\widetilde n=n-j\kappa.
$$

The real part, $n$, determines phase behavior; the **extinction coefficient**, $\kappa$, determines attenuation. It is directly related to $\alpha$ and is essential when analyzing plasmonic structures.

## 2.8 Dispersion and frequency-dependent material behavior

One of the most fascinating—and sometimes challenging—properties of real materials is that their electromagnetic parameters, including permittivity and permeability, depend on frequency rather than remaining constant. This is **dispersion**. Ignoring it in a broadband metasurface can produce substantial errors in predicted resonance and spectral response.

We use established physical models to describe material behavior across frequency:

### 2.8.1 The Debye model

As frequency increases, polar materials such as water and some biological polymers cannot keep pace with the changing field. The Debye model describes this response:

$$
\varepsilon(\omega)=\varepsilon_\infty+
\frac{\varepsilon_s-\varepsilon_\infty}{1+j\omega\tau}.
$$

Here $\varepsilon_s$ is static, zero-frequency permittivity, $\varepsilon_\infty$ is the high-frequency limit, and $\tau$ is the **relaxation time**. Loss rises strongly around the characteristic relaxation frequency.

### 2.8.2 Lorentz and Drude models

At higher frequencies—terahertz, infrared and visible—we turn to the physics of the material's particles:

- **Lorentz model:** describes dielectrics and bound electrons around atomic and molecular resonances. It explains strong absorption near particular frequencies.
- **Drude model:** a central model for optical metals such as gold and silver, describing free electrons. A remarkable consequence is that the real permittivity can become negative, $\varepsilon'<0$. This property underpins **plasmonics** and advanced optical metasurfaces capable of concentrating light below the wavelength scale.

## 2.9 Poynting vector and power flux

Understanding how fields transport energy is a central part of wave analysis. The **Poynting vector** describes energy-transfer rate per unit area: it gives the energy-flow direction and power flux. It is especially important in metasurfaces, where reflection, transmission, absorption and energy loss determine performance.

In the time domain, instantaneous Poynting vector is the field cross product:

$$
\mathbf S(t)=\mathbf E(t)\times\mathbf H(t).
$$

The fields $\mathbf E(t)$ and $\mathbf H(t)$ are instantaneous electric and magnetic fields. Their cross product tells us where energy moves at each instant and how much power crosses a unit area. Poynting-vector units are watts per square meter, $\mathrm{W/m^2}$.

In many engineering problems we use harmonic phasor fields with $e^{j\omega t}$. The quantity of greatest practical interest is then **time-averaged power**. Its Poynting vector is:

$$
\mathbf P_{\mathrm{ave}}=\frac12\operatorname{Re}\{\mathbf E\times\mathbf H^*\}.
$$

The star denotes complex conjugation, and $\operatorname{Re}\{\cdot\}$ takes the real part.

Taking the real part matters because $\mathbf E\times\mathbf H^*$ is generally complex. Its real part corresponds to net transported, active power. Its imaginary part is associated with reactive power: oscillatory energy exchange between fields and the medium without net energy transport.

In a uniform plane wave in a lossless medium, the fields are perpendicular and their amplitude ratio is set by intrinsic impedance. Average power density is then:

$$
\mathbf P_{\mathrm{ave}}=\frac{|\mathbf E_0|^2}{2\eta}\hat{\mathbf a}_n.
$$

Here $|\mathbf E_0|$ is electric-field amplitude and $\hat{\mathbf a}_n$ is the propagation unit vector. Average power density scales with electric amplitude squared and inversely with intrinsic impedance.

Equivalently, using the magnetic field:

$$
\mathbf P_{\mathrm{ave}}=\frac{\eta|\mathbf H_0|^2}{2}\hat{\mathbf a}_n.
$$

These expressions are equivalent in lossless media and are widely used for incident, transmitted and reflected powers.

### Power balance in metasurfaces

The Poynting vector is our main tool for **power balance** in a metasurface. When a wave arrives, some incident power is reflected, some transmitted, and some may be absorbed through ohmic, dielectric or resonant losses. Energy conservation gives:

$$
P_{\mathrm{inc}}=P_{\mathrm{ref}}+P_{\mathrm{tr}}+P_{\mathrm{abs}}.
$$

Here $P_{\mathrm{inc}}$ is incident power, $P_{\mathrm{ref}}$ reflected power, $P_{\mathrm{tr}}$ transmitted power, and $P_{\mathrm{abs}}$ absorbed power. Accordingly, power reflection, transmission and absorption coefficients are:

$$
R=\frac{P_{\mathrm{ref}}}{P_{\mathrm{inc}}},\qquad
T=\frac{P_{\mathrm{tr}}}{P_{\mathrm{inc}}},\qquad
A=\frac{P_{\mathrm{abs}}}{P_{\mathrm{inc}}}.
$$

For a passive structure, energy conservation requires their sum to equal one:

$$
R+T+A=1.
$$

These relations make quantitative performance assessment possible. For example:

- A **perfect metamaterial absorber** aims for vanishing transmission and reflection, making $A$ as close to one as possible.
- A **phase-controlling reflectarray** usually aims for $R$ close to one and $A$ as small as possible.

The Poynting vector therefore bridges field descriptions and energy analysis. Electric and magnetic fields describe local wave behavior; their Poynting vector shows how those fields carry and distribute energy through space.

## 2.10 Plane-wave polarization

**Polarization** is a fundamental electromagnetic-wave property. It describes how the direction and geometry of the electric-field vector evolve with time at a fixed point, or equivalently within an equiphase plane. Because many structures respond differently to different electric-field directions, polarization is central to antennas, filtering networks and especially metasurfaces.

Polarization control is an important form of metasurface field engineering. Cells can be designed to preserve, attenuate, separate or completely transform incident polarization. Polarization converters, selective surfaces, and co-/cross-polarized power control therefore all depend on polarization analysis.

### 2.10.1 Equiphase planes and component representation

For a plane wave propagating along $\hat{\mathbf a}_n$, phase depends on $\omega t-\beta\hat{\mathbf a}_n\cdot\mathbf r$. Surfaces satisfying the following condition are equiphase planes:

$$
\beta\hat{\mathbf a}_n\cdot\mathbf r=\mathrm{constant}.
$$

They are perpendicular to propagation, and temporal field changes have the same phase across each plane.

To study polarization, resolve electric field into two orthogonal components transverse to propagation. For propagation along $+z$, the instantaneous field can be written:

$$
\mathbf E(z,t)=\hat{\mathbf a}_x E_{0x}\cos(\omega t-\beta z+\phi_x)
+\hat{\mathbf a}_y E_{0y}\cos(\omega t-\beta z+\phi_y).
$$

The quantities $E_{0x}$ and $E_{0y}$ are component amplitudes; $\phi_x$ and $\phi_y$ are their initial phases. Their relative amplitudes and phases determine polarization: the path traced by the electric-vector tip over one temporal cycle in an equiphase plane.

### 2.10.2 Types of plane-wave polarization

We generally classify polarization into three main types:

**A) Linear polarization:** the field-vector tip oscillates along a straight line transverse to propagation. This occurs when the relative phase, $\Delta\phi=\phi_y-\phi_x$, is zero or an integer multiple of $\pi$:

$$
\phi_y-\phi_x=m\pi,\qquad m\in\mathbb Z.
$$

The component ratio is then constant. The field's direction stays on the same line while its magnitude and sign change with time. Horizontal, vertical and tilted polarizations are different linear cases.

**B) Circular polarization:** equal orthogonal component amplitudes with phase difference $\pm\pi/2$ make the tip trace a circle. Field magnitude remains constant while its direction rotates uniformly:

$$
E_{0x}=E_{0y},\qquad \phi_y-\phi_x=\pm\frac\pi2.
$$

Rotation direction distinguishes right-handed, RHCP, and left-handed, LHCP, circular polarizations. Because references differ in whether they look with or against wave propagation, every analysis should state its viewing convention explicitly.

**C) Elliptical polarization:** the general case. The component amplitudes differ, or their relative phase differs from zero, $\pi$ or $\pm\pi/2$, and the vector tip traces an ellipse. Linear and circular polarization can be regarded as special limiting cases of elliptical polarization.

Two parameters commonly describe its geometry:

- **Ellipse tilt, $\psi$:** the major-axis angle relative to the reference axis.
- **Ellipticity angle, $\chi$:** describes the minor-to-major-axis ratio and rotation sense.

### 2.10.3 Stokes parameters and the Poincaré sphere

**Stokes parameters**, commonly $S_0,S_1,S_2,S_3$, provide a compact, comprehensive description of total intensity and polarization. Their advantage is that polarization can be described without tracking instantaneous field phase directly.

Normalizing the Stokes parameters represents a polarization state as a point on a unit **Poincaré sphere**. In this geometric picture:

- The equator represents linear polarizations.
- The two poles represent opposite circular polarizations.
- Other surface points represent elliptical polarizations.

The sphere is useful for analyzing and tracking polarization transformations, comparing states, and interpreting anisotropic-metasurface responses.

### 2.10.4 Why polarization matters in metasurfaces and communication systems

Polarization choice or control is often the foundation of system operation rather than a secondary feature. A metasurface may respond very differently to different polarizations. Important applications include:

- **Polarization conversion:** linear to circular, circular to linear, or rotation between linear directions, including cross-polarization conversion.
- **Polarization separation:** structures transparent to one polarization and reflective or absorbing to its orthogonal partner.
- **Channel-capacity increase:** orthogonal polarizations can reduce interference and support polarization multiplexing, potentially doubling information channels.
- **Satellite communications:** circular polarization reduces sensitivity to relative antenna rotation and effects such as ionospheric Faraday rotation.
- **Polarimetric radar:** the reflected polarization-scattering matrix helps identify target geometry and material properties.

### 2.10.5 Polarization mismatch and polarization loss factor

If incident polarization does not match a receiving antenna, sensor or metasurface, not all incident power is received. This **polarization mismatch** reduces system efficiency.

For two linear polarizations separated by angle $\theta$, the **polarization loss factor, PLF**, is:

$$
\mathrm{PLF}=\cos^2\theta.
$$

This simple expression applies only to two linear polarizations. In general, PLF comes from the overlap, or inner product, of the incident and receiving complex polarization vectors.

In polarization-transforming metasurface analysis, the source describes maximizing the desired co-polarized component and minimizing unwanted cross-polarized power. **Cross-polarization ratio** and related purity measures quantify the performance of polarization-transforming surfaces.

The component amplitudes are unequal, $E_{0x}=4$ and $E_{0y}=3$, with relative phase $\Delta\phi=\pi/2$. The polarization is therefore **elliptical**.

## 2.11 Normal-incidence plane-wave reflection and transmission

### 2.11.1 Introduction

So far, our wave has traveled through an infinite homogeneous medium without interruption. Real optical and metasurface devices are full of boundaries. When a wave reaches a change in permittivity or permeability, it must divide its response: some energy returns to medium 1 as reflection, while some crosses into medium 2 as transmission.

Reflection and transmission underpin multilayer systems, antireflection coatings and especially metasurfaces. Our design art is to control their amplitudes, phases and polarizations. We begin with the simplest case: a plane wave normally incident on a flat interface.

### 2.11.2 Geometry and initial assumptions

Two linear, homogeneous, isotropic media meet at $z=0$. Medium 1 occupies the left half-space, $z<0$, and medium 2 the right half-space, $z>0$. Their electromagnetic properties are:

- **Medium 1:** intrinsic impedance $\eta_1$ and complex propagation constant $\gamma_1$.
- **Medium 2:** intrinsic impedance $\eta_2$ and complex propagation constant $\gamma_2$.

```{figure} ../assets/chapter-02/textbook-normal-incidence.svg
:alt: Redrawing of normal incidence: incident propagation toward the interface, reflected propagation away, and transmission into medium 2, with electric and magnetic directions indicated.

Normal-incidence geometry from source page 50. The boundary is $z=0$. Horizontal arrows show propagation; vertical arrows show electric field. Crosses indicate magnetic field into the page and dots out of it. With $x$ upward and $z$ rightward, positive $y$ points into the page. Original book redrawing with vector-consistent directions.
```

Suppose a uniform wave approaches the boundary from medium 1 along $+z$. At $z=0$, two new waves appear:

1. **Reflected wave:** returns through medium 1 along $-z$.
2. **Transmitted wave:** enters medium 2 and continues along $+z$.

### 2.11.3 Incident, reflected and transmitted phasors

Without losing generality for this setting, choose incident electric field along $\hat{\mathbf a}_x$ and magnetic field along $\hat{\mathbf a}_y$. With $e^{j\omega t}$, the incident phasors in medium 1 are:

$$
\widetilde{\mathbf E}_i(z)=\hat{\mathbf a}_x E_{i0}e^{-\gamma_1z},
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf H}_i(z)=\hat{\mathbf a}_y\frac{E_{i0}}{\eta_1}e^{-\gamma_1z}.
$$

The reflected wave travels along $-z$. Its fields are:

$$
\widetilde{\mathbf E}_r(z)=\hat{\mathbf a}_x E_{r0}e^{+\gamma_1z},
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf H}_r(z)=-\hat{\mathbf a}_y\frac{E_{r0}}{\eta_1}e^{+\gamma_1z}.
$$

:::{tip} Engineering sign check
The minus sign in reflected magnetic field is not a typo! Reversing propagation to $-z$ reverses the magnetic-field relation so that the Poynting vector, $\mathbf E\times\mathbf H$, follows the right-hand rule.
:::

Finally, transmission into medium 2 along $+z$ gives:

$$
\widetilde{\mathbf E}_t(z)=\hat{\mathbf a}_x E_{t0}e^{-\gamma_2z},
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathbf H}_t(z)=\hat{\mathbf a}_y\frac{E_{t0}}{\eta_2}e^{-\gamma_2z}.
$$

The unknown amplitudes are $E_{r0}$ and $E_{t0}$. Boundary conditions hold the key to finding them.

### 2.11.4 Deriving reflection and transmission from boundary conditions

At a simple boundary without free surface charge or current, the total tangential electric and magnetic fields are continuous at $z=0$:

$$
\left.\widetilde{\mathbf E}_{1,\mathrm{tan}}\right|_{z=0}
=\left.\widetilde{\mathbf E}_{2,\mathrm{tan}}\right|_{z=0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_{i0}+E_{r0}=E_{t0}.
$$

$$
\left.\widetilde{\mathbf H}_{1,\mathrm{tan}}\right|_{z=0}
=\left.\widetilde{\mathbf H}_{2,\mathrm{tan}}\right|_{z=0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{E_{i0}}{\eta_1}-\frac{E_{r0}}{\eta_1}=\frac{E_{t0}}{\eta_2}.
$$

Solving these two equations for the two unknowns yields the key parameters:

**Electric-field reflection coefficient, $\Gamma$:** the reflected-to-incident amplitude ratio:

$$
\Gamma\equiv\frac{E_{r0}}{E_{i0}}=\frac{\eta_2-\eta_1}{\eta_2+\eta_1}.
$$

This relation shows that, for this interface problem, reflection is caused by intrinsic-impedance mismatch, $\eta_1\ne\eta_2$.

**Electric-field transmission coefficient, $\tau_E$:** the transmitted-to-incident amplitude ratio:

$$
\tau_E\equiv\frac{E_{t0}}{E_{i0}}=\frac{2\eta_2}{\eta_2+\eta_1}.
$$

Comparing the formulas shows that $1+\Gamma=\tau_E$.

### 2.11.5 Power balance: reflected and transmitted energy

Alongside field amplitude, designers need transmitted and reflected powers. Time-averaged Poynting flux gives the **power reflectance, $R$**:

$$
R\equiv\frac{\langle S_r\rangle}{\langle S_i\rangle}
=|\Gamma|^2=\left|\frac{\eta_2-\eta_1}{\eta_2+\eta_1}\right|^2.
$$

In lossless media, **power transmittance, $T$**, is:

$$
T\equiv\frac{\langle S_t\rangle}{\langle S_i\rangle}
=\frac{\eta_1}{\eta_2}|\tau_E|^2
=\frac{4\eta_1\eta_2}{(\eta_1+\eta_2)^2}.
$$

Without material loss, energy conservation takes its familiar form:

$$
R+T=1.
$$

### 2.11.6 Special engineering cases

- **Perfect impedance matching, $\eta_1=\eta_2$:** here $\Gamma=0$ and $\tau_E=1$. The wave does not notice the boundary, and all power crosses without reflection, $R=0,T=1$.
- **Perfect electric conductor, PEC:** the ideal-metal impedance approaches zero, $\eta_2\to0$, giving $\Gamma=-1$. All power reflects, with $R=1,T=0$; no wave penetrates, and tangential electric field at the metal surface is zero.

### 2.11.7 Standing waves and wave impedance

The reflected wave means that the total field in medium 1 is no longer a single traveling wave. Interference between the forward and backward waves creates a spatial pattern whose field amplitude varies between maxima and minima. The **standing-wave ratio (SWR)** describes these variations:

$$
\mathrm{SWR}=\frac{|E_{\max}|}{|E_{\min}|}
=\frac{1+|\Gamma|}{1-|\Gamma|}.
$$

This ratio is 1 for perfect matching and infinite for reflection from an ideal metal.

Furthermore, in the presence of reflection the electric-to-magnetic field ratio is no longer simply $\eta_1$! We introduce a position-dependent **wave impedance ($Z_w$)**. If the positive distance from the boundary is $d=-z$, the input impedance follows the familiar transmission-line expression:

$$
Z_{\mathrm{in}}(d)=\eta_1
\frac{\eta_2+j\eta_1\tan(\beta_1d)}{\eta_1+j\eta_2\tan(\beta_1d)}.
$$

For an ideal metallic surface (PEC), $\Gamma=-1$ and a **complete standing wave** forms. The average transmitted power is zero, $\langle\mathbf S\rangle=0$, while energy exchanges between the electric and magnetic fields in space.

::::{admonition} Booklet example: normal incidence from air onto a dielectric
:class: book-exercise

A plane wave of frequency $f=100\,\mathrm{MHz}$ travels through free space (medium 1) and strikes a lossless dielectric with $\varepsilon_{r2}=4$ normally. Its incident electric field is:

$$
\mathbf E_i(z,t)=\hat{\mathbf a}_x10\cos(\omega t-\beta_1z)\,\mathrm{V/m}.
$$

:::{dropdown} Show / hide the solution
The impedances are:

$$
\eta_1=\eta_0\approx120\pi\,\Omega,
\qquad
\eta_2=\frac{\eta_0}{\sqrt4}=60\pi\approx188.5\,\Omega.
$$

The amplitude reflection and transmission coefficients are:

$$
\Gamma=\frac{60\pi-120\pi}{60\pi+120\pi}=-\frac13,
\qquad \tau_E=1+\Gamma=\frac23.
$$

The reflected and transmitted field amplitudes are:

The power coefficients are also straightforward to calculate:

$$

\qquad

$$

Energy conservation, $R+T=1$, is satisfied.

### 2.11.9 Connection to metasurface engineering

Our analysis so far has concerned natural material boundaries. **Metasurfaces** change what we can do at these boundaries! Instead of relying on bulk materials, we arrange an array of subwavelength unit cells on the interface.

These artificial structures induce electric and magnetic surface currents, $\mathbf J_s$ and $\mathbf M_s$, modifying the classical boundary conditions into generalized sheet transition conditions (GSTCs):

$$
\hat{\mathbf a}_z\times(\widetilde{\mathbf H}_2-\widetilde{\mathbf H}_1)=\mathbf J_s,
\qquad
(\widetilde{\mathbf E}_2-\widetilde{\mathbf E}_1)\times\hat{\mathbf a}_z=\mathbf M_s.
$$

By designing the geometry of these elements, we can control the effective surface impedance, $Z_s$, point by point. This capability enables, for example:

- **Perfect absorbers:** matching the surface input impedance to the free-space impedance, $\eta_1$, to eliminate reflection.
- **Phase-controlling metasurfaces:** applying a nonuniform surface phase to redirect reflected or transmitted wavefronts, as we will see in the **generalized Snell law** below.

:::{note} Matching and absorption are separate questions
Zero reflection establishes matching under the stated illumination. Perfect absorption additionally requires zero outgoing transmission and every other propagating output channel to vanish. A free-standing electric sheet is a two-port system; a ground-backed structure can act as a one-port system. Do not transfer the matching or absorption condition between them without accounting for the backing and its input impedance.
:::

### Computational connection: Add a film and keep the phase

Time convention in this computational connection: $e^{-i\omega t}$.


Can adding a material layer reduce reflection? At normal incidence, let a lossless film of index $n_f$ and thickness $d$ lie between media $n_0$ and $n_2$. Use the single-interface coefficients $r_{ab}=(n_a-n_b)/(n_a+n_b)$ and $t_{ab}=2n_a/(n_a+n_b)$, and define

$$
\delta=k_0n_fd=\frac{2\pi n_fd}{\lambda_0}.
$$

Successive round trips have a coherent phase factor $e^{2i\delta}$. Summing their geometric series, or solving the four boundary equations, gives

$$
r_{\mathrm{film}}=\frac{r_{0f}+r_{f2}e^{2i\delta}}{1+r_{0f}r_{f2}e^{2i\delta}},\qquad
t_{\mathrm{film}}=\frac{t_{0f}t_{f2}e^{i\delta}}{1+r_{0f}r_{f2}e^{2i\delta}}.
$$

The incident reference is at the entrance $z=0$; the transmitted amplitude is defined at the exit $z=d$. The fractions are $R=|r_{\mathrm{film}}|^2$ and $T=(n_2/n_0)|t_{\mathrm{film}}|^2$.

For the design wavelength $\lambda_d$, choose $n_f=\sqrt{n_0n_2}$ and $d=\lambda_d/(4n_f)$. Then $\delta=\pi/2$, the reflected contributions cancel, and $R=0$. The mechanism uses interference under specific conditions, not disappearance of the interfaces or absorption of the reflected beam.

## 2.12 Reflection and refraction at oblique incidence

### 2.12.1 Geometry and the wave vector

When the incidence angle relative to the surface normal is nonzero, the wave vector $\mathbf k$ describes propagation. Take the incidence plane as $xz$ and the interface as $z=0$:

- **Incident wave vector:** $\mathbf k_i=k_1(\hat{\mathbf a}_x\sin\theta_i+\hat{\mathbf a}_z\cos\theta_i)$.
- **Reflected wave vector:** $\mathbf k_r=k_1(\hat{\mathbf a}_x\sin\theta_r-\hat{\mathbf a}_z\cos\theta_r)$.
- **Transmitted (refracted) wave vector:** $\mathbf k_t=k_2(\hat{\mathbf a}_x\sin\theta_t+\hat{\mathbf a}_z\cos\theta_t)$.

Here $k_1=\omega\sqrt{\mu_1\varepsilon_1}$ and $k_2=\omega\sqrt{\mu_2\varepsilon_2}$.

### 2.12.2 Phase matching and the classical Snell laws

Field continuity at every point on the interface, $z=0$, requires identical spatial phase dependence along the interface coordinate $x$ for all three waves (**phase matching**):

$$
k_{ix}=k_{rx}=k_{tx}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
k_1\sin\theta_i=k_1\sin\theta_r=k_2\sin\theta_t.
$$

Two familiar laws of geometrical optics follow from this fundamental relation:

**1. Law of reflection:**

$$
\theta_r=\theta_i.
$$

**2. Snell's law of refraction:**

$$
n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t,
\qquad n=\sqrt{\varepsilon_r\mu_r}.
$$

### 2.12.3 Parallel (TM) and perpendicular (TE) polarizations

At oblique incidence, the fields depend on their polarization relative to the **plane of incidence**, which contains the surface normal and the wave vector:

- **Perpendicular polarization (TE or s):** the electric field $\mathbf E$ is perpendicular to the incidence plane, along $\hat{\mathbf a}_y$.
- **Parallel polarization (TM or p):** the magnetic field $\mathbf H$ is perpendicular to the incidence plane, while the electric field lies within it.

### 2.12.4 Fresnel coefficients

Applying the boundary conditions to tangential $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf H$ gives the Fresnel reflection and transmission coefficients.

For TE polarization:

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{TE}}=
\frac{\eta_2\cos\theta_i-\eta_1\cos\theta_t}
{\eta_2\cos\theta_i+\eta_1\cos\theta_t},
\qquad
\tau_{\mathrm{TE}}=
\frac{2\eta_2\cos\theta_i}{\eta_2\cos\theta_i+\eta_1\cos\theta_t}
=1+\Gamma_{\mathrm{TE}}.
$$

For TM polarization:

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{TM}}=
\frac{\eta_2\cos\theta_t-\eta_1\cos\theta_i}
{\eta_2\cos\theta_t+\eta_1\cos\theta_i},
\qquad
\tau_{\mathrm{TM}}=
\frac{2\eta_2\cos\theta_i}{\eta_2\cos\theta_t+\eta_1\cos\theta_i}.
$$

### 2.12.5 Brewster angle and total internal reflection

**Brewster angle ($\theta_B$, or polarizing angle):** the incidence angle at which the TM reflection coefficient vanishes, $\Gamma_{\mathrm{TM}}=0$. For two nonmagnetic dielectrics, $\mu_1=\mu_2=\mu_0$:

$$
\theta_B=\arctan\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_2}{\varepsilon_1}}
=\arctan\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right).
$$

At this angle, the reflected wave is entirely TE polarized.

**Critical angle and total internal reflection (TIR):** when a wave travels from a higher-index medium toward a lower-index medium, $n_1>n_2$, incidence beyond the critical angle, $\theta_i>\theta_c$, satisfies:

$$
\theta_c=\arcsin\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right).
$$

No power passes into the second medium and all incident power is reflected. The field in medium 2 becomes an **evanescent wave**, decaying exponentially along the interface normal, $+z$, while propagating along the surface, $+x$.

::::{admonition} Booklet example: TE oblique incidence
:class: book-exercise

A TE-polarized plane wave strikes a lossless dielectric obliquely from air. The interface is $z=0$ and the incidence plane is $xz$. The media have the following properties:

$$
\varepsilon_{r1}=1,\quad\mu_{r1}=1,\qquad
\varepsilon_{r2}=4,\quad\mu_{r2}=1.
$$

The incidence angle relative to the interface normal is $\theta_i=30^\circ$, and the incident electric-field amplitude is $E_{0i}=10\,\mathrm{V/m}$. Assuming both media are lossless:

1. Calculate each medium's refractive index and intrinsic impedance.
2. Find the reflection and refraction angles.
3. Find the TE electric-field reflection and transmission coefficients and the reflected and transmitted electric-field amplitudes.
4. Calculate the reflected and transmitted power fractions and check energy conservation.

:::{dropdown} Show / hide the solution
**1. Refractive indices and intrinsic impedances.** The refractive index is:

$$
n=\sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}.
$$

For medium 1, $n_1=\sqrt{(1)(1)}=1$, and for medium 2, $n_2=\sqrt{(1)(4)}=2$. Therefore:

$$
\boxed{n_1=1,\qquad n_2=2}.
$$

The intrinsic impedance is:

$$
\eta=\eta_0\sqrt{\frac{\mu_r}{\varepsilon_r}},\qquad \eta_0\simeq377\,\Omega.
$$

For medium 1:

$$
\eta_1=377\sqrt{\frac11}=377\,\Omega.
$$

For medium 2:

$$
\eta_2=377\sqrt{\frac14}=\frac{377}2=188.5\,\Omega.
$$

Consequently:

$$
\boxed{\eta_1=377\,\Omega,\qquad\eta_2=188.5\,\Omega}.
$$

**2. Reflection and refraction angles.** The reflection law gives $\theta_r=\theta_i=30^\circ$. Snell's law gives the refraction angle:

$$
n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t.
$$

Substituting the values:

$$
1\times\sin30^\circ=2\sin\theta_t.
$$

Since $\sin30^\circ=0.5$:

$$
\sin\theta_t=\frac{0.5}2=0.25.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\theta_t=\sin^{-1}(0.25),\qquad \boxed{\theta_t\simeq14.48^\circ}.
$$

The wave enters a higher-index medium and therefore bends toward the normal: $\theta_t<\theta_i$.

**3. TE reflection and transmission coefficients.** The TE electric-field reflection coefficient is:

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{TE}}=\frac{\eta_2\cos\theta_i-\eta_1\cos\theta_t}
{\eta_2\cos\theta_i+\eta_1\cos\theta_t}.
$$

Using $\cos30^\circ\simeq0.866$ and $\cos14.48^\circ\simeq0.968$:

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{TE}}
=\frac{(188.5)(0.866)-(377)(0.968)}{(188.5)(0.866)+(377)(0.968)}
=\frac{163.24-364.94}{163.24+364.94}
\simeq-0.382.
$$

The negative reflection coefficient indicates a $180^\circ$ phase reversal of the reflected electric field. For the tangential electric field in TE polarization:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{TE}}=1+\Gamma_{\mathrm{TE}}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{TE}}=1-0.382,\qquad\boxed{\tau_{\mathrm{TE}}\simeq0.618}.
$$

**4. Reflected and transmitted field amplitudes.** The reflected electric-field amplitude is:

$$
E_{0r}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{TE}}E_{0i}.
$$

Substituting:

$$
E_{0r}=(-0.382)(10),\qquad \boxed{E_{0r}\simeq-3.82\,\mathrm{V/m}}.
$$

The negative sign represents a $180^\circ$ phase difference relative to the incident field. Its magnitude is $|E_{0r}|\simeq3.82\,\mathrm{V/m}$.

The transmitted electric-field amplitude is:

$$
E_{0t}=\tau_{\mathrm{TE}}E_{0i}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
E_{0t}=(0.618)(10),\qquad\boxed{E_{0t}\simeq6.18\,\mathrm{V/m}}.
$$

**5. Power coefficients and conservation.** For lossless media, the reflected power fraction is the squared magnitude of the field reflection coefficient:

$$
R=|\Gamma_{\mathrm{TE}}|^2.
$$

Therefore:

$$
R=(-0.382)^2\simeq0.146,\qquad\boxed{R\simeq14.6\%}.
$$

The transmitted power fraction is not simply $|\tau_{\mathrm{TE}}|^2$, because both the impedance and propagation angle change. For TE polarization:

$$
T=\frac{\eta_1\cos\theta_t}{\eta_2\cos\theta_i}|\tau_{\mathrm{TE}}|^2.
$$

Substituting:

$$
T=\frac{(377)(0.968)}{(188.5)(0.866)}(0.618)^2
\simeq2.236(0.382)\simeq0.854.
$$

Approximately:

$$
\boxed{T\simeq85.4\%}.
$$

Checking energy conservation:

$$
R+T=0.146+0.854,\qquad\boxed{R+T=1}.
$$

Energy is conserved. Neither medium absorbs power, so $A=0$ and $R+T+A=1$.
:::

At Brewster incidence a surviving reflected wave is TE only if the input contains a TE component; a purely TM input has zero reflection. In lossless half-space total internal reflection, the transmitted net normal power is zero, while an evanescent field and tangential energy flux may remain. ### Computational connection: Derive the interface response

Time convention in this computational connection: $e^{-i\omega t}$.


Take the plane of incidence to be $xz$. **TE** has electric field along $y$. **TM** has magnetic field along $y$ and electric field in the $xz$ plane. We compare the same tangential electric component on each side: $E_y$ for TE and $E_x$ for TM. Define

$$
s=n_i\sin\theta_i,\qquad q_j=\frac{k_{z,j}}{k_0}=\sqrt{n_j^2-s^2},
$$

choosing the outgoing/decaying root, with nonnegative real and imaginary parts for these lossless positive-index half-spaces. Our dimensionless tangential wave admittance is

$$
Y_j^{\mathrm{TE}}=q_j,\qquad Y_j^{\mathrm{TM}}=\frac{n_j^2}{q_j}.
$$

The physical admittance is $Y_j/\eta_0$. To see the signs, use the signed tangential magnetic component $h=-H_x$ for TE and $h=H_y$ for TM. A forward wave has $h=YE_{\mathrm{tan}}/\eta_0$; a backward wave has the opposite relation. With incident tangential electric amplitude one, continuity gives

$$
1+r=t,\qquad Y_i(1-r)=Y_t t.
$$

Solving these two equations yields the Fresnel coefficients in our basis:

$$
\boxed{r=\frac{Y_i-Y_t}{Y_i+Y_t}},\qquad
\boxed{t=\frac{2Y_i}{Y_i+Y_t}}.
$$

For a propagating incident channel, $Y_i$ is real and positive. Its normal power fractions are

$$
R=|r|^2,\qquad T=\frac{\operatorname{Re}Y_t}{Y_i}|t|^2.
$$

For a lossless single interface, $R+T=1$. At normal incidence, $Y_j=n_j$ for both polarizations: from air to glass, $r=-0.2$, $t=0.8$, $R=0.04$, and $T=0.96$.

Some references define reflected TM amplitude along a polarization unit vector that rotates with the reflected wave. Here the reflected and incident coefficients both multiply a common tangential $x$ direction. Their amplitude signs can therefore differ from those references while the physical vector fields and powers agree. Compare conventions before treating a sign difference as an error. At normal incidence, our TE and TM tangential-electric coefficients agree.

## 2.13 Generalized Snell law

The classical **Snell law** assumes phase continuity at the interface between homogeneous media. Metasurfaces allow an **abrupt phase shift** across a thickness much smaller than a wavelength. This idea was formulated in 2011 by Capasso and colleagues, introducing the **generalized Snell law**.

Suppose a metasurface lies between media with refractive indices $n_i$ and $n_t$, imposing a spatial phase profile $\Phi(x)$ along the interface. The surface phase gradient, $d\Phi/dx$, contributes additional momentum to phase matching. The relation between incidence angle $\theta_i$ and refraction angle $\theta_t$ becomes:

$$
n_t\sin\theta_t-n_i\sin\theta_i=\frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi}\frac{d\Phi}{dx}.
$$

Here $\lambda_0$ is the vacuum wavelength. In terms of the wave-number magnitudes, $k_i$ and $k_t$, the engineering form is:

$$
k_t\sin\theta_t-k_i\sin\theta_i=\frac{d\Phi}{dx}.
$$

Similarly, reflection into the first medium, of refractive index $n_i$, obeys:

$$
\sin\theta_r-\sin\theta_i=\frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi n_i}\frac{d\Phi}{dx}.
$$

**Physical interpretation:** the $d\Phi/dx$ term shows how a designed phase gradient redirects reflected and transmitted beams (**anomalous reflection/refraction**). This principle underlies flat lenses, beam deflectors, and holographic metasurfaces.

::::{admonition} Booklet example: phase gradients and anomalous angles
:class: book-exercise

A phase-gradient metasurface lies between air and a dielectric. Let $n_1=1$, $n_2=1.5$, and $\lambda_0=10\,\mathrm{mm}$. A wave arrives at $\theta_i=20^\circ$. The imposed phase gradient along $x$ is:

$$
\frac{d\Phi}{dx}=100\,\mathrm{rad/m}.
$$

Use the generalized Snell law to find:

1. The anomalous reflection angle $\theta_r$.
2. The anomalous refraction angle $\theta_t$.
3. The phase difference between adjacent unit cells separated by $p=5\,\mathrm{mm}$.
4. The reflection and refraction angles when the phase gradient is zero.

:::{dropdown} Show / hide the solution
**Wavelength conversion.** First convert millimeters to meters: $\lambda_0=10\,\mathrm{mm}=0.01\,\mathrm m$. Also, $\sin20^\circ\simeq0.342$.

**Anomalous reflection angle.** The generalized reflection law is:

$$
\sin\theta_r-\sin\theta_i=\frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi n_1}\frac{d\Phi}{dx}.
$$

Solving for $\sin\theta_r$:

$$
\sin\theta_r=\sin\theta_i+\frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi n_1}\frac{d\Phi}{dx}.
$$

Substituting the given values:

$$
\sin\theta_r=\sin20^\circ+\frac{0.01}{2\pi(1)}(100).
$$

The phase-gradient contribution is:

$$
\frac{0.01\times100}{2\pi}=\frac1{2\pi}\simeq0.159.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\sin\theta_r=0.342+0.159\simeq0.501.
$$

Consequently:

$$
\theta_r=\sin^{-1}(0.501),\qquad\boxed{\theta_r\simeq30.1^\circ}.
$$

The phase gradient changes the reflection angle from its classical value of $20^\circ$ to approximately $30.1^\circ$.

**Anomalous refraction angle.** The generalized refraction law is:

$$
n_2\sin\theta_t-n_1\sin\theta_i=\frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi}\frac{d\Phi}{dx}.
$$

Solving for $\sin\theta_t$:

$$
n_2\sin\theta_t=n_1\sin\theta_i+\frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi}\frac{d\Phi}{dx}.
$$

Substituting the values:

$$
1.5\sin\theta_t=(1)(\sin20^\circ)+\frac{0.01}{2\pi}(100).
$$

Therefore:

$$
1.5\sin\theta_t=0.342+0.159=0.501.
$$

Consequently:

$$
\sin\theta_t=\frac{0.501}{1.5}\simeq0.334.
$$

Thus:

$$
\theta_t=\sin^{-1}(0.334),\qquad\boxed{\theta_t\simeq19.5^\circ}.
$$

The anomalous refraction angle is approximately $19.5^\circ$.

For comparison, the classical Snell law without a phase gradient gives:

$$
n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_{t,\mathrm{classic}},
\qquad
\sin\theta_{t,\mathrm{classic}}=\frac1{1.5}\sin20^\circ\simeq0.228.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\theta_{t,\mathrm{classic}}\simeq13.2^\circ}.
$$

Thus, the phase gradient changes the refraction angle from $13.2^\circ$ to approximately $19.5^\circ$.

**Phase difference between neighboring cells.** For cells separated by $p$:

$$
\Delta\Phi=\frac{d\Phi}{dx}p.
$$

The cell spacing is $p=5\,\mathrm{mm}=0.005\,\mathrm m$, so:

$$
\Delta\Phi=(100)(0.005),\qquad\boxed{\Delta\Phi=0.5\,\mathrm{rad}}.
$$

Expressed in degrees:

$$
\Delta\Phi=0.5\times\frac{180}\pi,\qquad\boxed{\Delta\Phi\simeq28.6^\circ}.
$$

The required phase difference between adjacent cells is 0.5 radians, approximately $28.6^\circ$.

**Without a phase gradient.** Set $d\Phi/dx=0$.

The reflection law becomes:

$$
\sin\theta_r-\sin\theta_i=0.
$$

Thus $\sin\theta_r=\sin\theta_i$, giving:

$$
\boxed{\theta_r=\theta_i=20^\circ}.
$$

The refraction law becomes:

$$
n_2\sin\theta_t-n_1\sin\theta_i=0,
\qquad n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t.
$$

This is the classical Snell law. Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\theta_t\simeq13.2^\circ}.
$$

**Results.** With $d\Phi/dx=100\,\mathrm{rad/m}$:

$$
\boxed{\theta_r\simeq30.1^\circ},\qquad
\boxed{\theta_t\simeq19.5^\circ},\qquad
\boxed{\Delta\Phi=0.5\,\mathrm{rad}\simeq28.6^\circ}.
$$

Without a phase gradient:

$$
\boxed{\theta_r=20^\circ},\qquad \boxed{\theta_t\simeq13.2^\circ}.
$$

The imposed phase gradient adds an effective tangential component to the wave vector and changes the reflection and refraction angles from their classical values.
:::
::::

:::{note} An allowed direction still needs an efficiency
A phase gradient predicts a candidate tangential wave number. A propagating angle exists only when the resulting sine lies between -1 and 1. Reflection and transmission generally have separately defined phase profiles; equal gradients in the example are an assumption. Signs depend on the phase convention. The law alone does not determine efficiency, unwanted diffraction, or coupling. See [Yu and colleagues' original 2011 paper](https://capasso.seas.harvard.edu/publications/light-propagation-phase-discontinuities-generalized-laws-reflection-and) and Chapters 4–5 for the periodic and full-wave checks.
:::

## 2.14 Generalized sheet transition conditions (GSTCs)

Classical interface boundary conditions treat the boundary as having zero thickness, relating field jumps directly to electric and magnetic surface currents, $\mathbf J_e$ and $\mathbf J_m$. Although metasurfaces are thin compared with the wavelength, their scattering elements (meta-atoms) have physical thickness and develop electric and magnetic dipole moments when illuminated.

For a macroscopic model, we use **generalized sheet transition conditions (GSTCs)**. The metasurface is represented by an infinitesimally thin sheet with effective electric and magnetic surface susceptibility tensors, $\overline{\overline\chi}_{ee}$ and $\overline{\overline\chi}_{mm}$.

The electric and magnetic field discontinuities across the metasurface relate to equivalent surface currents as follows:

$$
\hat{\mathbf n}\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf J_e,
\qquad
(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)\times\hat{\mathbf n}=\mathbf J_m.
$$

Here $\hat{\mathbf n}$ points from medium 1 to medium 2. The key feature of the GSTC constitutive model is that $\mathbf J_e$ and $\mathbf J_m$ depend on the **average fields** on the two sides and on the surface susceptibility tensors:

$$
\mathbf J_e=j\omega\varepsilon_0\overline{\overline\chi}_{ee}\mathbf E_{\mathrm{av}}
+j\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}\overline{\overline\chi}_{em}\mathbf H_{\mathrm{av}},
$$

$$
\mathbf J_m=j\omega\mu_0\overline{\overline\chi}_{mm}\mathbf H_{\mathrm{av}}
+j\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0}\overline{\overline\chi}_{me}\mathbf E_{\mathrm{av}}.
$$

In these equations:

- $\mathbf H_{\mathrm{av}}=(\mathbf H_1+\mathbf H_2)/2$ and $\mathbf E_{\mathrm{av}}=(\mathbf E_1+\mathbf E_2)/2$ are the average fields on the two sides.
- $\overline{\overline\chi}_{ee}$ and $\overline{\overline\chi}_{mm}$ describe direct electric and magnetic responses.
- $\overline{\overline\chi}_{em}$ and $\overline{\overline\chi}_{me}$ describe magnetoelectric cross-coupling, which can appear in chiral or asymmetric metasurfaces and is often taken as zero in suitably symmetric structures.

### Why GSTCs matter in design

GSTCs connect macroscopic physics to microscopic metasurface design. We first determine the susceptibility tensors needed for a desired response, such as reflection, absorption, or transmission with a chosen phase. We then optimize the unit-cell geometry to realize those susceptibilities, as later chapters explore.

:::{note} The reduced GSTC model and its normal
Here the normal points from medium 1 toward medium 2, opposite to Section 2.4. The displayed susceptibility equations are a reduced tangential-polarization model. General GSTCs also contain tangential gradients of normal surface polarization and magnetization. Anisotropy and magnetoelectric coupling are distinct properties; symmetry conclusions require the actual geometry, environment, and illumination. The derivation by [Holloway and Kuester](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09582) provides a primary reference for the homogenized interface model.
:::

### Computational connection: Give a sheet a current

Time convention in this computational connection: $e^{-i\omega t}$.


Consider an ideal, local, purely electric surface at normal incidence. Let its sheet admittance be $Y_s$ in siemens, and define the dimensionless quantity $g=\eta_0Y_s$. Because there is no magnetic surface current, tangential electric continuity still gives $t=1+r$. The electric jump condition adds a shunt current:

$$
Y_i(1-r)-Y_t t=g\,t.
$$

Solving yields

$$
r=\frac{Y_i-Y_t-g}{Y_i+Y_t+g},\qquad
t=\frac{2Y_i}{Y_i+Y_t+g},\qquad
A=\frac{4Y_i\operatorname{Re}g}{|Y_i+Y_t+g|^2}.
$$

Here $Y_i=n_i$ and $Y_t=n_t$ at normal incidence. For $\operatorname{Re}g\geq0$, the sheet is passive and $R+T+A=1$. The absorbed power follows directly from $\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}Y_s|E_{\mathrm{sheet}}|^2$, rather than from unexplained missing channels.

This is also an equivalent-circuit language: the wave regions are transmission-line ports and $Y_s$ is a shunt at their junction. Under $e^{-i\omega t}$, a capacitance per square has $Y_s=-i\omega C_s$ and an inductance has $Y_s=+i/(\omega L_s)$. Positive conductance dissipates power. An engineering convention using $e^{+i\omega t}$ reverses these reactive signs.

For equal air ports and real $g=G\geq0$,

$$
A=\frac{4G}{(2+G)^2}\leq\frac12,
$$

with equality at $G=2$. A larger conductance eventually reflects more strongly instead of absorbing more. This 50% limit belongs to this one-sided, purely electric, equal-medium sheet model. A backed sheet, added magnetic response, different environment, or different illumination changes the problem.

For the equal-air electric shunt at the common sheet plane,

g=\frac{2(1-t)}{t}.

Before applying it, check the complex continuity relation $t=1+r$, the reference planes, the exterior impedances, and the model assumptions. Near $t=0$, small errors in $t$ can produce large parameter errors; at $t=0$ this inversion is undefined. A passive model should also satisfy the appropriate energy constraints.

An arbitrary finite film or patterned ridge does not automatically meet those conditions. In particular, entrance and exit planes of a finite layer are different planes. ## 2.15 Scattering matrices and S-parameters in metasurfaces

At microwave and optical frequencies, directly measuring voltages and currents can be difficult or impossible. We therefore use **scattering parameters**, or **S-parameters**, to analyze high-frequency networks and periodic structures such as metasurfaces. They relate the incident and outgoing traveling waves at the network ports.

A metasurface simulation commonly uses a single **unit cell** with periodic boundary conditions, representing the structure as a **two-port network**:

- **Port 1:** the side receiving the incident wave.
- **Port 2:** the side through which the transmitted wave leaves.

The scattering matrix is defined by:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}V_1^-\\V_2^-\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}S_{11}&S_{12}\\S_{21}&S_{22}\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}V_1^+\\V_2^+\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Here $V_n^+$ is the wave amplitude entering port $n$, and $V_n^-$ is the outgoing (reflected or transmitted) amplitude. Two parameters are particularly useful:

### 1. Reflection coefficient ($S_{11}$)

This is the reflected-to-incident wave ratio at port 1 when port 2 is matched, $V_2^+=0$:

$$
S_{11}=\frac{V_1^-}{V_1^+}=|S_{11}|e^{j\angle S_{11}}.
$$

- **Magnitude ($|S_{11}|$):** describes the reflected response, corresponding to the transmission-line coefficient $\Gamma$. Reflectarrays often aim for $|S_{11}|$ close to 1, or 0 dB.
- **Phase ($\angle S_{11}$):** describes the reflected-wave phase. Changing the meta-atom dimensions changes this phase, allowing the phase gradients used in generalized Snell-law designs.

### 2. Transmission coefficient ($S_{21}$)

This is the wave leaving port 2 relative to the wave incident at port 1:

$$
S_{21}=\frac{V_2^-}{V_1^+}=|S_{21}|e^{j\angle S_{21}}.
$$

- **Magnitude ($|S_{21}|$):** describes the transmitted response, corresponding to $\tau$. Transmitarrays generally aim to maximize transmission.
- **Phase ($\angle S_{21}$):** controls the transmitted-wave phase and thus the wavefront on the other side of the metasurface.

### Connection to absorption

Energy conservation gives the absorbed power fraction, $A$, from the S-parameter magnitudes:

$$
A=1-|S_{11}|^2-|S_{21}|^2.
$$

An absorber aims to minimize both reflection, $S_{11}$, and transmission, $S_{21}$, bringing absorption toward 100 percent.

S-parameters also support the retrieval of effective properties, such as $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$. Later chapters examine these procedures and their simulation requirements.

::::{admonition} Booklet example: from scattering parameters to power
:class: book-exercise

A metasurface unit cell is simulated as a two-port network. At the operating frequency, $|S_{11}|=0.2$ and $|S_{21}|=0.8$. Only port 1 is illuminated, and port 2 is matched, $V_2^+=0$. Calculate:

1. The percentage of reflected power.
2. The percentage of transmitted power.
3. The percentage of absorbed power.
4. Whether the metasurface is predominantly transmitting or absorbing, explaining your answer.

:::{dropdown} Show / hide the solution
**1. Reflected power.** The reflection-amplitude magnitude is $|S_{11}|$. The reflected power fraction is:

$$
R=|S_{11}|^2.
$$

Substituting the given value:

$$
R=(0.2)^2=0.04.
$$

As a percentage:

$$
\boxed{R=4\%}.
$$

Thus, 4 percent of the incident power is reflected.

**2. Transmitted power.** The transmitted power fraction is:

$$
T=|S_{21}|^2.
$$

Therefore:

$$
T=(0.8)^2=0.64.
$$

As a percentage:

$$
\boxed{T=64\%}.
$$

Thus, 64 percent of the incident power passes through the metasurface.

**3. Absorption.** When reflection and transmission are the only outgoing channels:

$$
A=1-|S_{11}|^2-|S_{21}|^2.
$$

Substituting:

$$
A=1-(0.2)^2-(0.8)^2=1-0.04-0.64=0.32.
$$

The absorbed percentage is:

$$
\boxed{A=32\%}.
$$

**4. Energy conservation.** Reflected, transmitted, and absorbed power fractions must sum to one:

$$
R+T+A=1.
$$

Substituting the results:

$$
0.04+0.64+0.32=1.
$$

Therefore, $R+T+A=1$ and energy is conserved.

**5. Predominant response.** Transmission accounts for 64 percent, reflection for 4 percent, and absorption for 32 percent. Most power passes through the structure, so the response is predominantly **transmitting**, with some absorption.

For an ideal transmitting metasurface, $|S_{21}|$ should approach one while $|S_{11}|$ and absorption decrease. For an ideal absorber, both $|S_{11}|$ and $|S_{21}|$ should approach zero.

**Final answer:**

$$
\boxed{R=4\%},\qquad \boxed{T=64\%},\qquad\boxed{A=32\%}.
$$

The structure is predominantly **transmitting**.
:::
::::

:::{note} Magnitude is an amplitude, not a power fraction
For properly power-normalized propagating port waves, reflected and transmitted fractions are the squared magnitudes of the relevant S-parameters. The magnitudes themselves are amplitude ratios. Raw electric transmission coefficients require the corresponding admittance ratio. The two-term absorption formula applies only when those are all outgoing channels: include other polarizations and diffraction orders before identifying missing power as absorption. The two-port example assumes power-wave normalization, a matched receiving port, and no other outgoing modes.
:::

### Computational connection: Normalize a scattering matrix

Time convention in this computational connection: $e^{-i\omega t}$.


For propagating ports with real positive admittance, scale each incoming/outgoing tangential field by $\sqrt{Y_j/(2\eta_0)}$. The resulting amplitudes have squared magnitude equal to normal power flux. Write

$$
\mathbf b=S\mathbf a,\qquad P_{\mathrm{in}}=\mathbf a^\dagger\mathbf a,\qquad
P_{\mathrm{out}}=\mathbf b^\dagger\mathbf b.
$$

A lossless complete scattering description requires $S^\dagger S=I$. A passive one requires $\mathbf a^\dagger(I-S^\dagger S)\mathbf a\geq0$ for every input. Reciprocity and mirror symmetry are separate properties; a substrate can break the latter without breaking the former.

For our bare interface between two propagating channels,

$$
S=\begin{bmatrix}r&\tau\\\tau&-r\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\tau=\frac{2\sqrt{Y_iY_t}}{Y_i+Y_t}.
$$

The opposite reflection signs follow our field bases and port arrangement. $S$ is symmetric and unitary. Raw electric-field transmission coefficients from opposite sides need not be equal; their power-normalized counterparts are equal here. An evanescent solution with zero normal flux cannot be normalized as an independent unit-power propagating port by dividing by $\sqrt{\operatorname{Re}Y}$.

## 2.16 Equivalent-circuit modeling of metasurfaces

Full-wave numerical tools such as CST or HFSS provide detailed electromagnetic analysis, but they can be time-consuming and may not directly explain a structure's physical behavior. When the unit-cell size is much smaller than the operating wavelength, $a\ll\lambda$, quasistatic approximations can support **lumped equivalent-circuit models** of meta-atoms.

We map physical features to resistance ($R$), inductance ($L$), and capacitance ($C$):

- **Inductance ($L$):** metallic current paths have inductive behavior. A longer or narrower path generally increases the equivalent inductance.
- **Capacitance ($C$):** gaps between adjacent metal regions and dielectric substrates contribute capacitance.
- **Resistance ($R$):** represents metal ohmic losses and substrate dielectric losses.

### Resonance frequency

Many meta-atoms, including split-ring resonators and crossed patches, resonate near a characteristic frequency. A series or parallel RLC circuit approximates this response, with resonance frequency:

$$
f_r=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}.
$$

This useful relation suggests how to lower the resonance frequency: lengthen the current path to increase $L$, or reduce gaps to increase $C$.

### Transmission-line model

To analyze propagation through a metasurface, represent free space or the surrounding dielectric as a transmission line of characteristic impedance $Z_0$. In free space, $Z_0=120\pi\approx377\,\Omega$. The sheet is then represented by a shunt impedance, $Z_{\mathrm{ms}}$, or shunt admittance, $Y_{\mathrm{ms}}$.

Once the equivalent impedance is known, transmission-line theory gives reflection and transmission (the S-parameters). For example, the reflection coefficient, $\Gamma$ or $S_{11}$, for a structure illuminated from free space is:

$$
S_{11}=\frac{Z_{\mathrm{in}}-Z_0}{Z_{\mathrm{in}}+Z_0}.
$$

Here $Z_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the input impedance of the combined metasurface and the structure behind it.

Circuit modeling is a useful tool for initial predictions, rapid optimization, and analytical design before detailed numerical simulation.

::::{admonition} Booklet example: RLC resonance and geometry changes
:class: book-exercise

A split-ring meta-atom is represented by a series RLC circuit with $L=4\,\mathrm{nH}$, $C=1\,\mathrm{pF}$, and $R=50\,\Omega$. The surrounding free-space impedance is $Z_0=377\,\Omega$. Using the equivalent-circuit model, calculate:

1. The initial resonance frequency.
2. The series-circuit impedance at resonance.
3. The reflection coefficient at resonance.
4. The reflected power percentage at this frequency.
5. The new resonance frequency if geometry changes increase the inductance to $L'=9\,\mathrm{nH}$ while keeping capacitance fixed, and interpret the change.

:::{dropdown} Show / hide the solution
**1. Initial resonance frequency.** For the RLC circuit:

$$
f_r=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}.
$$

First convert to SI units: $L=4\,\mathrm{nH}=4\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm H$ and $C=1\,\mathrm{pF}=1\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm F$. Therefore:

$$
f_r=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{(4\times10^{-9})(1\times10^{-12})}}.
$$

The product is $LC=4\times10^{-21}$, giving:

$$
\sqrt{LC}=2\times10^{-10.5}\simeq6.325\times10^{-11}.
$$

Consequently:

$$
f_r=\frac1{2\pi(6.325\times10^{-11})},\qquad\boxed{f_r\simeq2.52\,\mathrm{GHz}}.
$$

**2. Circuit impedance at resonance.** The series RLC impedance is:

$$
Z_{\mathrm{RLC}}=R+j\left(\omega L-\frac1{\omega C}\right).
$$

At resonance, the imaginary part vanishes because $\omega_rL=1/(\omega_rC)$. Thus $Z_{\mathrm{RLC}}=R$, giving:

$$
\boxed{Z_{\mathrm{RLC}}=50\,\Omega}.
$$

The circuit is purely resistive: the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel.

**3. Reflection coefficient.** Assuming that the structure's input impedance equals the RLC impedance:

$$
\Gamma=S_{11}=\frac{Z_{\mathrm{in}}-Z_0}{Z_{\mathrm{in}}+Z_0}.
$$

At resonance $Z_{\mathrm{in}}=50\,\Omega$, so:

$$
S_{11}=\frac{50-377}{50+377}=\frac{-327}{427},
\qquad\boxed{S_{11}\simeq-0.766}.
$$

The negative sign indicates a $180^\circ$ reflected-wave phase reversal. The magnitude is $|S_{11}|\simeq0.766$.

**4. Reflected power percentage.** The reflected power fraction is:

$$
R=|S_{11}|^2.
$$

Therefore:

$$
R=(0.766)^2\simeq0.587,\qquad\boxed{R\simeq58.7\%}.
$$

Approximately 58.7 percent of the incident power is reflected at resonance.

**5. New resonance frequency.** With $L'=9\,\mathrm{nH}=9\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm H$ and unchanged $C=1\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm F$:

$$
f_r'=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{L'C}}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
f_r'=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{(9\times10^{-9})(1\times10^{-12})}}.
$$

Now $L'C=9\times10^{-21}$ and $\sqrt{L'C}\simeq9.487\times10^{-11}$. Therefore:

$$
f_r'=\frac1{2\pi(9.487\times10^{-11})},\qquad\boxed{f_r'\simeq1.68\,\mathrm{GHz}}.
$$

**6. Interpretation.** The resonance decreases from $f_r\simeq2.52\,\mathrm{GHz}$ to $f_r'\simeq1.68\,\mathrm{GHz}$. Thus **increasing inductance lowers the resonance frequency**, consistent with:

$$
f_r\propto\frac1{\sqrt L}.
$$

Physically, lengthening or narrowing metallic current paths can increase the meta-atom's equivalent inductance, shifting its response to lower frequencies.

**Final answer:**

$$
\boxed{f_r\simeq2.52\,\mathrm{GHz}},\quad
\boxed{Z_{\mathrm{RLC}}=50\,\Omega},\quad
\boxed{S_{11}\simeq-0.766},\quad
\boxed{R\simeq58.7\%}.
$$

Increasing the inductance from 4 to 9 nanohenries gives:

$$
\boxed{f_r'\simeq1.68\,\mathrm{GHz}}.
$$

Increasing $L$ therefore shifts the resonance toward lower frequencies.
:::
::::

:::{note} A resonator impedance is not automatically a sheet input impedance
The source RLC example explicitly assumes that the whole one-port input impedance equals the series-resonator impedance. Under that assumption its reflection calculation is valid. For an unbacked shunt sheet, however, the load seen at the input is the parallel combination of the sheet and the transmitted-medium admittance. The computational sheet derivation in Section 2.14 gives that two-port result. A resonance estimate also needs a valid circuit topology and material model before it predicts a physical device.
:::

### Computational connection: Where did the missing light go?

Return to the air-surrounded sheet with $g=2$. One incident beam gives $r=-1/2$, $t=1/2$, and $A=1/2$. Now illuminate it coherently from both sides, with total incoming power normalized to one:

$$
\mathbf a=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\e^{i\phi}\end{bmatrix},\qquad

$$
